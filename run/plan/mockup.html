Who Gets the Suffix?

For anybody who's curious, no linguistics background (novice) · about 8 minutes of the reader's time · scrollytelling with three interactive moments · ToC: yes (desktop rail, collapsed on mobile)

Standfirst (draft): "She slept." "She saw him." Your gut says the sleeper and the seer are the same kind of thing. In about a third of the languages that mark case on nouns, grammar disagrees. Eight minutes, a few taps, one small Basque ending.

Shape: The page is about one distinction: who gets grouped with whom. So its core is a single "alignment lens" canvas, and its highlight visibly jumps from S+A (English) to S+P (made-up "ergative English", then Basque) as you scroll. After that the reader decodes real Basque by tapping -k on and off, flips a Hindi tense switch, and reads a WALS chart. Every flashy element (the morphing outline, the snapping -k, the growing bars) does a teaching job. I rejected a free-form sandbox, because novices need the S/A/P names first, and a plain article, because the flip is far stronger seen than read.

Visual identity: bold and playful (the builder's call). One fixed rule: three role colours, S, A and P, plus one accent for the ending "-k". They mean the same thing in every figure, and each chip also carries its letter.

Objectives:

  • o-1 Explain S, A and P with English sentences, and show that English pronouns group S with A. checks: k-01, k-02, k-03
  • o-2 Given Basque sentences, predict which nouns take ergative -k and which stay bare (absolutive): S groups with P. checks: k-04, k-05, k-06
  • o-3 Explain that ergativity is often partial (split), and that neither grouping is "more logical". checks: k-07, k-08

check_plan: PASS, no warnings (3 objectives, 6 segments, 3 widgets, 8 checks, 13 acceptance criteria).

Accuracy notes carried into the plan: The request lists "Basque, Kurdish, or Georgian" as ergative, but Kurdish and Georgian are split systems, and the page says so (s-05). Every Basque sentence comes from a verified, published set. Nouns in the puzzles are marked singular, because Basque "-ak" can also mean plural.

Segments

Prose is draft wording: it shows what each segment says and in what order. The final prose is written for the reader at build time. Check prompts, options and answers are final.

s-01

Which two belong together?

hook → predicto-1

Get the reader to commit to their own instinct before any jargon, so the page can overturn it later.

"Three people, two sentences. Tap the two that feel like the same kind of thing."

No terms yet. The response appears in place and the pick is remembered (in page memory only) for the ending.

PREDICT · k-01
Tap the two that feel like "the same kind of thing": the sleeper ("She slept"), the one who sees ("She saw him"), or the one who is seen ("She saw him").
Options: sleeper + seer / sleeper + seen / seer + seen. There's no wrong answer. Feedback: "Hold on to your pick. Most English speakers choose the sleeper and the seer, because English says 'she' for both. Some languages choose the sleeper and the seen."

s-02

Three roles, and English's grouping

B1 step-locked · concrete → named → applyo-1

Pre-train S, A and P as three chunks on familiar English, and show that English gives S and A the same pronoun form.

Step 1: "Look at who's in each sentence. 'She slept' has one participant. 'She saw him' has two: one who acts and one who's acted on."

Step 2: The chips gain letters and colours. "Linguists call these S (the single participant), A (the agent) and P (the patient, the one acted on)."

Step 3: The outline wraps S and A. "English uses 'she' for both S and A, and switches to 'him/her' only for P. That grouping is called nominative-accusative." Collapsed aside: "Why not just say subject and object?"

APPLY · k-02
"He fell." and "He pushed her." Which pronoun is S, which is A, which is P, and which two share a form?
Options: A) S = He, A = He, P = her: S and A share "he" · B) S = He, A = her, P = He: S and P share "he" · C) S = her, A = He, P = He. Correct: A. Feedback explains that the role comes from who does what to whom.

Row 2 "She saw him." with chips "She" (A, subject form) and "him" (P, object form). A rounded outline encloses S + A and is labelled "same form" on the outline itself. A mode label in the corner reads "English". Step 1 shows no letters; at step 2 the letters and colours fade in. An aria-live line describes the grouping. s-03

Flip it: an "ergative English"

predict → reveal · contrasting caseo-1

Let the reader feel S+P grouping with their own pronouns, and flag clearly that this English is made up.

"Now imagine an English that draws the line somewhere else, grouping the sleeper with the one who is seen."

After the commit, the outline slides off A onto P, and "She slept" morphs into "Her slept". One sentence: "Same three roles, different grouping. Real ergative languages usually do something neater: they leave S and P alone and give the agent an extra ending. Here's one."

PREDICT · k-03 (gates the lens morph)
Imagine an English that groups the sleeper with the one who is seen, not with the seer. How would "She slept" come out?
Options: A) She slept. · B) Her slept. · C) She slept her. Correct: B. The tempting answer is A (real English).
WIDGET · w-alignment-lens · state "Ergative English (made up)" Same canvas. Row 1 now reads "Her slept." (S, object form). Row 2 is unchanged: "She saw him." The outline animates (about 500ms) from S+A to S+P, labelled "same form". A persistent tag reads "Made-up English: no one speaks this". A mode switch (English / Ergative English) lets the reader flip back and forth. The morph is reader-triggered and respects reduced motion.
s-04

Real ergativity: decode some Basque

B3 worked → contrast → faded completiono-2

Show that Basque leaves S and P bare and marks A with -k, then have the reader produce the forms. Confront "it's just a passive".

Lens, last state: "Martin etorri da." 'Martin has arrived.' / "Martinek Diego ikusi du." 'Martin has seen Diego.' "Same Martin. He gets an ending only when he does something to someone. Diego, the one seen, stays bare, just like Martin who arrived."

Rule, stated once: "The doer of a two-participant verb gets -k (-ek after a consonant): the ergative. Everyone else stays bare: the absolutive. S and P together, A on its own."

Umea table, then the decoder. Collapsed asides: "Wait, couldn't -ak mean plural?" (gizonak = 'the man' as doer, or 'the men') and "Met 'ergative verbs' in English class?" (verbs like break use the word in a different sense, and having them doesn't make English ergative).

APPLY · k-04 (decoder puzzle 1)
'The woman has come.' → [Emakumea | Emakumeak] etorri da. Correct: Emakumea. Feedback introduces clue 2: "da" means nobody has -k.
APPLY · k-05 (decoder puzzles 2–3)
'The woman has read the book.' → [Emakumea | Emakumeak] [liburua | liburuak] irakurri du. Correct: Emakumeak liburua.
'The man has seen the woman.' → [Gizona | Gizonak] [emakumea | emakumeak] ikusi du. Correct: Gizonak emakumea.
Feedback names the error: -k on P means "you grouped P with A, English-style"; a bare doer means "check the du".
COMPARE · k-06
Someone says "Martinek Diego ikusi du" is really a passive, like "Diego was seen by Martin", since Martin gets the extra marking. Are they right?
Options: A) Yes: marking the doer means it's a passive · B) No: it's an ordinary active sentence, 'Martin has seen Diego' · C) Only in the past tense. Correct: B. Tempting: A.

s-05

Not all-or-nothing: split ergativity

predict → toggle → B4 cardso-3

Show that ergativity is often partial, and correct the "Basque, Kurdish, Georgian are all ergative" summary.

"Plenty of languages are ergative only some of the time. Hindi-Urdu is a good place to watch it switch on."

After the toggle: "Same boy, same book. Only the tense changed, and the ergative marker switched on. This is split ergativity."

Then three cards: "You may have read that Kurdish and Georgian are ergative languages. That's half true."

PREDICT · k-07 (unlocks the switch)
Hindi-Urdu has an ergative ending, -nē. "The boy sells a book." vs "The boy has sold a book." Where does -nē show up on the boy?
Options: A) In both · B) Only in "has sold" · C) Only in "sells" · D) In neither. Correct: B. Tempting: A (all-or-nothing).
WIDGET · w-split-switch · complexity low + FIGURE · three language cards Switch: a two-segment control [sells | has sold]. "sells": laṛkā · pustak · bēctā hai (boy · book · sells), with the agent chip "A: no marker". "has sold": laṛkē-nē · pustak · bēcī hai (boy-ERG · book · has sold), with -nē in the accent colour. Footnote: the noun itself shifts laṛkā → laṛkē. Locked until k-07.
Cards (side by side; stacked at 360px; not a carousel): Kurdish: "accusative in the present, ergative in past-tense transitives". Georgian: "ergative ('narrative') case mainly in the past (aorist) series, and even then some lone-participant verbs take it: it sorts verbs by agency". Dyirbal (Australia): "nouns ergative, but 'I' and 'you' accusative". Each card has a mini on/off badge for where ergativity applies.
s-06

How common is each, and which is "logical"?

chart → callback → explain → recapo-3

Give the real numbers, return to the reader's opening pick, and land the point that both groupings are architectures, not logic.

"Out of 190 languages surveyed for case on nouns, most mark none of these roles at all. Of those that do, accusative edges out ergative, 52 to 32."

Callback: "At the start you grouped [the sleeper and the seer]. That's English's grammar talking."

Close: the recap strip, plus one line naming what's beyond this page: tripartite and active systems (both in the chart), and syntactic ergativity, where grammar rules beyond endings follow the S+P grouping.

EXPLAIN · k-08 (think, then reveal)
Why isn't English's grouping (sleeper with seer) more logical than Basque's (sleeper with seen)?
Model answer: each grouping picks out something real. English groups the main participant who starts things. Basque groups whoever the event simply happens to, and marks only an agent acting on someone else. Same three roles, different line, and both are common.

Predict · no wrong answer

Tap the two that feel like “the same kind of thing”: the sleeper (“She slept”), the one who sees (“She saw him”), or the one who is seen (“She saw him”).

No wrong answer here. Hold on to your pick. Many English speakers pick the sleeper and the seer, because English uses “she” for both. By the end you’ll meet languages whose grammar picks the sleeper and the seen.

Three roles, and English’s grouping

Let’s put those three people under a lens. Scroll on, and watch the canvas as you go.

English · 1 of 3

Start with who’s in each sentence. She slept has one participant, the sleeper, and nobody else. She saw him has two: one who does the seeing, and one who gets seen.

English · 2 of 3

Linguists give these three roles one-letter names. They’ve just appeared on the canvas.

S is the single participant of a one-participant verb like slept.

A is the agent: the doer of a two-participant verb like saw.

P is the patient: the one the action lands on. (Some books call it O, for object.)

English · 3 of 3

Now look at the words themselves. S and A are both she. Only P gets a different form: him, or her. So English draws its line around S and A, and leaves P on its own.

This grouping is called nominative-accusative, or accusative for short.

Why not just say “subject” and “object”?

Because “subject” already bundles S and A together, and that bundle is exactly what this page puts on trial. Three separate letters let us ask which two a language groups.

Also, English shows the grouping only on pronouns. A noun like the man looks the same whether he sleeps, sees, or is seen.

Apply

“He fell.” and “He pushed her.” Which pronoun is S, which is A, which is P, and which two share a form?

Now the real thing: decode some Basque

Basque

Two Basque sentences: Martin etorri da, ‘Martin has arrived’, and Martinek Diego ikusi du, ‘Martin has seen Diego’.

Same Martin in both. When he simply arrives, his name has no ending. When he does something to someone, he becomes Martin-ek. And Diego, the one seen, stays bare, just like Martin who arrived.

The rule

That’s the whole trick. In Basque, the doer of a two-participant verb (A) gets the ending -k, spelled -ek after a consonant, as in Martinek. This ending is called the ergative.

The one acted on (P) stays bare, and so does the lone participant (S) of verbs like ‘arrive’ and ‘come’. The bare form is called the absolutive. S goes with P, and A stands apart: Basque is an ergative-absolutive language.

The canvas switch now flips between all three views. Compare where the outline sits in English and in Basque.

One pair of sentences could be a fluke. Here is one noun, umea ‘the child’, in all three roles.

Wait, couldn’t -ak mean plural?

Good catch, and it’s a real trap. The -a on these nouns works like “the”. Add the doer’s -k and you get gizonak, ‘the man’ as a doer. But gizonak is also how Basque says ‘the men’ in the plain, unmarked form. Only the rest of the sentence tells you which one you’re looking at.

Met “ergative verbs” in an English class?

Some English grammar books call verbs like break “ergative verbs”, because they work both ways: it broke the window and the window broke. That’s a different use of the same word, and linguists haven’t settled on one name for those verbs.

Having verbs like break doesn’t make English ergative. On this page, “ergative” means an ending that the doer of a two-participant verb wears, as in Basque.

Not all-or-nothing

So far it might sound as if every language picks one grouping and sticks to it. Many don’t. Hindi-Urdu has its own doer ending, -nē, and it’s a good place to watch a language change its mind.

Predict

Hindi-Urdu has an ergative ending, -nē. “The boy sells a book.” vs “The boy has sold a book.” Where does -nē show up on the boy?

Same boy, same book. Only the tense changed, from ‘sells’ to ‘has sold’, and the doer’s ending switched on with it. A language that is ergative in some tenses and accusative-looking in others has split ergativity.

That clears up a popular claim: that Kurdish and Georgian are ergative languages, just like Basque. Each is only partly ergative, and each splits in its own way. Dyirbal, from Australia, splits along a different line altogether: by the kind of word.

How common is each, and which is “logical”?

Is ergativity a rare oddity, then? The World Atlas of Language Structures (WALS) checked how 190 languages mark these roles on ordinary nouns.

Just over half the languages, 98 of 190, don’t mark these roles on their nouns at all. Among the 92 that do, the accusative grouping is more common, 52 to 32. But ergative is no curiosity: it’s about a third of them.

Explain · think, then reveal

Why isn’t English’s grouping (sleeper with seer) more logical than Basque’s (sleeper with seen)?

Give yourself a moment to answer in your head first.

Each grouping picks out something real. English groups by “who the sentence is about, who starts things”: the sleeper and the seer are both the main participant. Basque groups by “who the event happens to”: the sleeper and the seen are both simply undergoing or being in the event, and only an agent acting on someone else gets marked.

Both use the same three roles. They draw the line in different places, and both are common in the world’s languages: 52 accusative and 32 ergative in the WALS sample, while just over half mark neither on nouns. Your instinct from the start of the page is your language’s architecture, not universal logic.

## Claims

### c-01
objective: o-1
claim: Linguists label three core roles: S is the single participant of an intransitive verb, A is the agent of a transitive verb, and O (also written P, for patient) is its object.
source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ergative–absolutive_alignment
quote: "A is the agent of a transitive verb. O is the object of a transitive verb, also written P for 'patient.' S is the single core argument of an intransitive verb."
confidence: high
note: Quote is the fetch tool's rendering of the article's definitions, not a character-exact copy. Dixon writes O, Comrie writes P; either works for novices if introduced once.

### c-02
objective: o-1
claim: English pronouns show accusative alignment: "He found me" and "He traveled" use the same form for A and S, while the object gets a different form (me/him).
source: https://static.hlt.bme.hu/semantics/external/pages/morfológia_1/en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ergative%e2%80%93absolutive_language.html
quote: "He (A) found me (O)." / "He (S) traveled." / "(S form = A form)"
confidence: high
note: Archived copy of an earlier Wikipedia version. Only pronouns show this in English; full nouns ("the man") are unmarked for all three roles.

### c-03
objective: o-2
claim: In the Basque sentence "Martin etorri da" ('Martin has arrived'), the lone participant Martin takes no ending: it is in the unmarked absolutive.
source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ergative–absolutive_alignment
quote: "Martin etorri da." Gloss: Martin-ABS, has arrived. Translation: "Martin has arrived."
confidence: high
note: Archived version glosses it "Martin-Ø", making the zero ending explicit.

### c-04
objective: o-2
claim: In "Martinek Diego ikusi du" ('Martin has seen Diego'), the agent gets the ergative ending (Martin-ek) while the person seen, Diego, stays unmarked like the lone arriver.
source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ergative–absolutive_alignment
quote: "Martinek Diego ikusi du." Gloss: Martin-ERG, Diego-ABS, has seen. Translation: "Martin has seen Diego."
confidence: high
note: This is an ordinary active sentence, not a passive. Archived version translates it "Martin saw Diego".

### c-05
objective: o-2
claim: Basque's ergative case, marked with -k, is for the transitive subject; the absolutive covers both the intransitive subject and the transitive direct object.
source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basque_grammar
quote: The nuclear cases table gives the ergative as "transitive subject" and the absolutive as "intransitive subject; transitive direct object." The case morpheme table lists the ergative as "-k."
confidence: high
note: Surface forms vary (-k after a vowel, -ek after a consonant as in Martin-ek, and combined with the article: -ak, -ek). Safe simplification for novices: "the agent gets -k."

### c-06
objective: o-2
claim: Basque gizonak is ambiguous: it can mean 'the man' as an agent (singular ergative) or 'the men' as plain absolutive, because article -a plus ergative -k looks like plural -ak.
source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ergative–absolutive_alignment
quote: "gizon-a (man-the.sing.abs), gizon-ak (man-the.pl.abs), gizon-ak (man-the.sing.erg), gizon-ek (man-the.pl.erg)"
confidence: high
note: Context (verb agreement) disambiguates. Any puzzle using "gizonak" must account for this or avoid it.

### c-07
objective: o-2
claim: In WALS's sample of 190 languages, 32 mark full nouns ergatively, about 52 accusatively, and 98 make no case distinction at all.
source: https://wals.info/chapter/98
quote: "Neutral 98 | Nominative–accusative (standard) 46 | Nominative–accusative (marked nominative) 6 | Ergative–absolutive 32 | Tripartite 4 | Active–inactive 4 | Total 190"
confidence: high
note: Counts only case marking on full noun phrases (Comrie, WALS 98A), not verb agreement or pronouns. WALS notes hotbeds in Australia and the Caucasus, near-absence in Africa, rarity in Europe.

### c-08
objective: o-3
claim: Kurdish is split by tense: transitive sentences are nominative-accusative in the present but ergative in the past.
source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kurdish_grammar
quote: "Transitive verbs show nominative/accusative marking in the present tense, and ergative marking in the past tense."
confidence: high
note: So "Kurdish is ergative" in the original request is only half true. Kurmanji vs Sorani mechanisms differ (case vs clitics) — not verified in detail; avoid specifics.

### c-09
objective: o-3
claim: Georgian uses its ergative (called "narrative") case mainly in the aorist (past) series; in other tenses its subjects are not ergative-marked.
source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georgian_grammar
quote: the ergative "generally only occurs in the aorist series"; Georgian has "often been said to exhibit split ergativity" and "mostly behaves like an ergative–absolutive language in the Series II ('aorist') screeves."
confidence: high
note: The same article says this is "not a fully accurate representation": in the aorist some intransitive verbs also take ergative subjects, so it "follows an active alignment" of the "split-S" type. Safe one-liner: "Georgian is ergative-ish only in the past, and even then sorts verbs by agency."

### c-10
objective: o-3
claim: Hindi-Urdu marks the agent with ergative -nē only in the perfective: "laṛkē-nē pustak bēcī hai" ('the boy has sold a book') versus unmarked "laṛkā pustak bēctā hai" ('the boy sells a book').
source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Split_ergativity
quote: "laṛkē-nē pustak bēcī hai." gloss "boy:MASC.SG.ERG book:FEM.SG.NOM sell:PRF.FEM.SG be:3P.SG.PRS" 'The boy has sold a book.' / "laṛkā pustak bēctā hai." gloss "boy:MASC.SG.NOM ... sell:HAB.MASC.SG ..." 'The boy sells a book.'
confidence: high
note: Also note verb agreement switches: perfective bēcī agrees with feminine 'book', habitual bēctā with masculine 'boy'.

### c-11
objective: o-3
claim: Dyirbal (Australia) marks nouns ergatively, but its first- and second-person pronouns ("I", "you") follow an accusative pattern.
source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Split_ergativity

confidence: high

### c-12
objective: o-1
claim: English "ergative verbs" like break ("He broke the window" / "The window broke") are a different, loose use of the word; linguists also call them labile or ambitransitive verbs.
source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ergative_verb
quote: labile verbs can also be called "ergative," following Lyons's 1968 textbook, or "S=O-ambitransitive," following Dixon's usage. "The terminology in general linguistics is not stable yet."
confidence: medium
note: The connection is real (the window is S in one, O in the other, i.e. S=O), but having such verbs does not make English an ergative language. "Different use" is our framing; the source says the term is used "in most other contexts ... for ergative constructions."
